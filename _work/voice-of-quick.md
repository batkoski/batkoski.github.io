## The problem

Amazon Quick needed to sound like itself, not like a standard AWS console. And it had two very different kinds of words to get right: the static copy in the interface, and the responses the AI writes on the fly.

There were far more teams shipping copy than there were writers to review it. Reviewing string by string wasn't going to scale.

## How I approached it

First, unblock the launch. I put together initial copy guidance based on the AWS style guidelines so teams had something to work from right away.

Then I tried to find the voice instead of inventing it. I ran a personality survey with leadership and users. The results were clear: serious over playful, professional over friendly. That shaped a principle I kept coming back to. For an AI product, personality doesn't come from adding adjectives or enthusiasm. It comes from being smarter and more helpful. When the product isn't smart, people stop trusting all of it.

## What I made

A voice and tone guide for static UI copy, and a separate one for the AI's own responses, with examples for each surface.

Then a way for teams to use it without me. I built a UX copy assistant: an AI agent trained on the voice guidance that writes publish-ready copy from a design screenshot, a text file, or a plain description. In its first three months, 21 people used it across more than 200 conversations, and other teams started using it as the reference for their own AI agents' tone.
