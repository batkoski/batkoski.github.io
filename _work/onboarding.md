## The problem

When a product can do a lot, the instinct is to explain all of it up front. Connect your tools, import your files, set your preferences, take the tour. Every one of those screens is reasonable on its own. Together they push the moment someone actually gets value further away.

What we saw pointed the other way: the people who stuck around were the ones who had a genuinely useful first conversation. So the question became how to get someone there as fast as possible, with as little in the way as we could manage.

## How I approached it

I wrote it out as a decision tree before anyone drew screens. What do we already know about this person when they arrive? What's the smallest thing we need to ask? What happens when we don't know enough?

The answer I proposed was context as a ladder, not a single bet. Use what we already have first, then ask for one thing (like their role), then offer to bring in more, and fall back to a good default when none of that is available. If we already know enough, skip the screen entirely.

We also looked at how other chat-first products handle the first minute. The pattern that held up was the simplest one: one page that covers the essentials, then drop people straight into chat.

I also rewrote the starter prompts people see in that first chat. The titles are in the user's voice, and the descriptions are in the assistant's. “Get a well-researched answer” became “Research a topic in depth for me,” which reads like something you'd actually ask.

And one principle for every empty state in the flow: an empty state is never a dead end. It always explains what's going on and offers a real next step.
